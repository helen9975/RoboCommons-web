Motivation

How do we add new scenarios as capabilities grow?

  1. Policies are becoming generalists. Robot learning is shifting from specialized policies toward models that follow diverse instructions, manipulate many objects, and operate across a growing range of environments.
  2. Evaluation has remained static. Benchmarks have been critical for measuring progress, but a finite task suite can only test the capabilities and failure modes anticipated by its designers.
  3. Evaluation should evolve alongside policies. With RoboCommons, users describe the behaviors they wish to evaluate in natural language, and the resulting tasks accumulate in a shared repository.

Abstract

Generalist robotic manipulation policies are rapidly expanding in capability, but their evaluation remains largely tied to fixed benchmarks designed and defined at benchmark design time. We introduce RoboCommons, a language-driven multi-framework harness for open-ended evaluation that lets users specify what they want to test while handling framework-specific task implementation. RoboCommons generates executable, verified tasks across existing manipulation frameworks and accumulates them into a reusable evaluation repository. Using more than 300 generated tasks, we show that evaluation diversity continues to grow as the repository expands. Its automated verifier produces tasks that are 98.5% correct under human adjudication while retaining 93.6% of valid tasks. Across three frameworks, RoboCommons is rated significantly more usable and less cognitively demanding compared to GenSim and Cursor in a 26-participant user study. Finally, across 15 policy–framework combinations and 42,576 episodes, targeted task families reveal distinct capability gaps that are obscured by aggregate success rates. These results show that robotic evaluation can be made easier to author, reliable to execute, and extensible as policy capabilities evolve.

    Method

    From a language request to a verified, framework-native task

    A user specifies a behavior and selects a framework, scene and assets. A VLM agent, scaffolded with skills and simulator tools, defines the instruction, relevant assets and surfaces, goal condition G and randomized initial state. Before a task is admitted to the repository, it must compile, run in the simulator, settle without collisions, remain reachable, and satisfy G(sinitial) = 0 and G(sgoal) = 1.

    1. 1

      Describe

      The user describes the behavior they want to evaluate in natural language and selects a framework, scene, fixtures and objects.

    2. 2

      Ground

      The agent defines the policy instruction, task-relevant assets and goal condition G, then measures the usable support surfaces within the robot's workspace. Templates cover supported relations, and the agent authors new logic when they do not apply.

    3. 3

      Place

      Assets are placed in measured regions on those surfaces, and a seeded reset procedure samples initial states within the robot's reach.

    4. 4

      Verify

      The task must compile, run in simulation, settle without collisions, be reachable through inverse kinematics, and satisfy G = 0 at the start and G = 1 at the goal across seeded resets. Failed checks trigger repair and revalidation.

    5. 5

      Share

      The validated task joins the shared repository, where it runs in its framework's native simulator against any policy.

    Three frameworks, one workflow

    RoboCommons builds on existing simulation infrastructure

    Rather than replacing the simulation infrastructure the community has already built, RoboCommons provides a common authoring workflow and delegates execution to framework-specific backends. To connect a new framework, the agent reads its code and examples and exposes native operations for scene loading, asset discovery, simulation, success checking and evaluation.

    One shared task repository

    Growth and diversity

    Source breadth expands coverage at a fixed task budget

    We measure the diversity of the 300 tasks with the Vendi score, an effective count of distinct samples, over four aspects: language instruction, scene and goal appearance, goal-relative visual change, and joint motion. At a fixed budget of 100 tasks, drawing from three domains instead of one increases diversity on every aspect. The gain is smallest for language, so instruction diversity captures only part of the expanded coverage.

    Diagnostic evaluation

    Task families distinguish shared limitations from policy-specific profiles

    We organize tasks into five capability families and evaluate eight generalist policies across 15 policy–framework combinations and 42,576 episodes. Overall success does not exceed 24.8% in any combination, but family-level scores reveal distinct profiles. Semantic grounding is a shared limitation, with no policy above 1.5% in any framework, while clutter produces the largest between-policy spread, up to 66 percentage points.

    Linked variations

    Linked variations show where performance deteriorates

    Because tasks are authored from language, it is inexpensive to write families of linked variants that hold the goal fixed and vary a single factor. These comparisons provide finer resolution than family averages.

    Simulation-to-real validation

    Relative task difficulty agrees across simulation and hardware

    We reconstruct a real tabletop scene in RoboCommons, matching its object set, layout and camera pose, and evaluate MolmoAct2 and π0.5 on six tasks in both simulation and the real world. The results provide initial evidence that relative difficulty can persist across simulation and hardware for the tested tasks.
